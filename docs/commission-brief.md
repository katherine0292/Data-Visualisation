## Purpose and audience

Harbourline Education Consulting commissions an English-language data visualisation story for people considering study in Sydney. The opening is a familiar news headline about record-high international student numbers. A prospective student may read it as a signal that admission has become more competitive, visas harder to obtain or daily life harder to afford. Those questions require different measures and different geographic levels. The story should help a reader identify what to check before choosing a course, intake and location.

The primary reader is represented by **Mei**, a 23-year-old considering a Master's degree in Sydney. She must decide whether the plan is feasible, when to apply, where to study and how much flexibility to allow for rising prices. Begin with her interpretation of the headline. Give her a reason to consult each chart, show how the comparison changes her thinking and end with a plan she can discuss with her family.

The secondary reader is represented by **Daniel**, an education adviser who briefs prospective students. Give him chart captions and method notes he can cite outside the presentation. A standalone caption should identify the measure, geographic scope, observation window, source and the main interpretation limit. Where a chart compares constructed measures, explain how they are calculated.

## Narrative and interaction

Build ten illustrated scenes in four narrative stages.

1. **Setting, scenes 1 and 2.** Mei sees the headline and asks whether it describes the intake she would join. Use Figure 1 to distinguish enrolments from commencements.
2. **Investigation, scenes 3 through 7.** Mei locates study hubs and filters her search by field with Figures 2 and 3. Figures 4 and 5 help her distinguish historical visa settings from the composition of her education sector. Figure 6 helps her reason about price pressure.
3. **Synthesis, scene 8.** Mei considers intake, study location, visa timing and affordability together. Bring the numbered figures back into view so the audience can trace each insight.
4. **Decision and outcome, scenes 9 and 10.** With her family, Mei identifies four next steps. Check application dates and relevant official requirements, shortlist campus areas for her field, allow a buffer in her own budget and compare intake using sector commencement shares. The illustrated arrival resolves the story as a persona scenario.

The browser presentation should open from a local HTML file, support keyboard and button navigation in both directions, reveal comparisons in meaningful stages and provide speaker notes. Keep the interaction usable during a live presentation. The companion film should communicate the core decision arc in a linear format.

## Figure specifications and acceptance criteria

| Figure and story placement | Required treatment | Review check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Enrolments and commencements** · Scenes 1–2 | Plot both NSW course series using January–May year-to-date counts for each year from 2019 to 2026. Index each series to its January–May 2019 value, set to 100. Label the two series directly. | A viewer can identify the enrolment line as the one referenced by the headline and explain why the commencement series can follow a different path. Captions identify course registrations and new course starts as the units. |
| **2. Sydney study hubs** · Scene 3 | Map disclosed full-year 2025 course enrolments across Sydney Statistical Areas Level 4 (SA4s). Use a logarithmic colour scale and identify the largest hub and top-three concentration. | A viewer can locate the major hubs, explain the denominator used for concentration and describe the map as a view of study locations. |
| **3. Region and field of study** · Scene 4 | Use a log-colour heatmap covering 14 Sydney SA4s and 13 fields. Show disclosed counts and keep suppressed or unavailable combinations blank. | A viewer can name one or two areas to investigate for a chosen field. The legend identifies blanks as missing or suppressed. |
| **4. Visa grant rates and policy dates** · Scene 5 | Align the national monthly Subclass 500 grant rate and three-month moving average with selected policy milestones. Label the national scope and make the two scales legible. | A viewer can match historical policy changes to the relevant periods and describe the timeline as context. The chart gives no individual visa probability or causal estimate. |
| **5. New and continuing enrolments by sector** · Scene 6 | Use stacked bars for Higher Education, Vocational Education and Training (VET) and ELICOS. Calculate continuing registrations as enrolments less commencements and label the new share for each sector. | A viewer can identify which displayed sector has the most enrolments and which has the highest new share. The view describes the composition of the snapshot. |
| **6. Price pressure and enrolment trends** · Scene 7 | Compare NSW enrolments with the Sydney All Groups and Rents CPI series on rebased scales. Explain the series-specific 2019 baselines and flag 2026 as a partial year. | A viewer can describe how prices and enrolments changed and explain why a price index cannot supply a personal dollar budget. |

## Data and documentation

Use published aggregate data from the Australian Government Department of Education, the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) and the Department of Home Affairs. The five inputs are the monthly international student tables, enrolment and commencement data by SA4, ABS Edition 3 SA4 boundaries, Student Visa Program data and the ABS Sydney CPI series. Keep the geography explicit. The comparisons use NSW course activity, Sydney study locations, Sydney price measures and national visa decisions.

Record the observation period, frequency, units, transformations and source for each figure. In particular, preserve the suppression used in the study-location data, distinguish course registrations from unique people and indicate where annual averages use a mix of quarterly and monthly observations. Treat policy annotations as historical context. Document the geographic and methodological limits alongside the figures wherever they could change the reader's interpretation.
